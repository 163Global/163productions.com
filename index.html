Live broadcasts for desert and off-road racing. Cameras, commentary, tracking and onboard cameras, run by people who race.

Tell us about your race
Late light in the desert. Most of a race happens where nobody is standing.

Somewhere past race mile 300, a mother is watching a GPS dot that hasn't moved in eleven minutes.

Then a camera finds the truck, and it's moving again. That is the job. We broadcast the whole race for the people who can't be standing on the course, from the start line to the last car in.

Behind the dust, there's a room like this.

Course cameras, onboard cameras and spectators' phones all feed one switcher. A producer picks the shot. The booth calls the race. The leaderboard and map update on air from live GPS.

It all goes out as one show, to YouTube, Facebook or your own site.

What race day looks like when we run it

Most crews show up with cameras. Everything here comes with our show, wired into the same broadcast.

  1. The day before

    We install onboard cameras during tech inspection. Teams rent them from us, so nobody buys gear for one race.

    Car numbers, classes and driver names come straight from registration. The graphics are right before the first car rolls.

  2. The start

    Commentary goes live from the booth. The leaderboard is on screen from the first timing point.

  3. Out on course

    Every car carries GPS, and the course map updates on air. When a car stops moving, we usually know why, and we say so.

    Spectators can join the show with our StreamEasy app. Their phones become extra cameras at the pits and crossings, and our producer picks which ones go live.

  4. The Monday after

    Every sponsor gets a report showing how long they were on screen, and when. Teams get their own clips for social media. The full replay goes up for everyone else.

We stay on after dark.

A lot of broadcasts wind down once the leaders finish. Ours stays on until the last car is in.

Those are the hardest miles in the race. They're also the ones families are watching.

We race these courses too.

We also build the registration systems and websites some of these races run on. So we see the details other crews never see.

We know why a class starts where it does, and we don't put the wrong number on screen. We know a stopped dot can mean a flat, a pit stop or a dead zone. We know what a team needs from a broadcast: footage for their sponsors, and proof their car was seen.
